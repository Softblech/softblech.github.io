Über den SongAbout the song

„How to Feel" ist ihre neue Single — und sie trifft einen sehr spezifischen Schmerz: den emotionalen Schwebezustand zwischen dem Wissen, dass eine Beziehung vorbei ist, und der Unfähigkeit, loszulassen. Kein dramatischer Bruch, keine laute Auflösung. Stattdessen der gedehnte Moment davor: die stille Erkenntnis, dass sich Liebe kalt anfühlt, dass man an etwas festhält, das man nicht mehr will — und dass die Person, die man immer noch liebt, vielleicht nie die richtige war.

Der Titel ist keine Aussage, sondern eine echte Frage: Wie fühlt man etwas, das nie ganz real war? Wie trauert man um das, was man nie wirklich hatte?

Der Refrain kreist um genau dieses Paradox — „How to feel / What we never had / Can't be saved again" — und kehrt immer wieder zurück, jedes Mal ein bisschen schwerer. „How to breathe." „How to see." Die grundlegendsten Dinge werden ungewiss, wenn der emotionale Boden unter einem nachgibt.

Die Bridge bringt einen kurzen, zerbrechlichen Moment der Erleichterung: Tränen kommen, das Kämpfen hört auf — und irgendwo unter all den Zweifeln taucht, unerwartet, so etwas wie Befreiung auf. „I feel good again." Nicht triumphierend. Einfach ehrlich.

"How to Feel" is their new single — and it sits in a very specific kind of pain: the emotional limbo between knowing a relationship is over and still being unable to let go. It doesn't dramatize a breakup. It inhabits the drawn-out moment before it: the quiet realization that love has gone cold, that you're holding on to something you no longer want, that the person across from you was never quite the one — even if part of you never stopped loving them.

The title isn't a statement. It's a genuine question. How do you feel something that was never fully real? How do you grieve what you never truly had?

The refrain circles this paradox relentlessly — "How to feel / What we never had / Can't be saved again" — each pass landing a little heavier. "How to breathe." "How to see." The most fundamental things become uncertain when the emotional ground shifts beneath you.

The bridge offers a brief, fragile moment of release: the tears come, the trying stops, and somewhere beneath all the doubt — unexpectedly — there's relief. "I feel good again." Not triumphant. Just honest.

Interview

Who is SOFTBLECH?

Dennis: We're a two-piece from Berlin that startet in Hamburg: Henri on guitar and vocals, me on bass and vocals. We've been writing and performing together for 3 years now, building a sound that lives somewhere between a wave aesthetic and postpunk influences. We start to call it Postwave.

What is "How to Feel" about?

Henri: That's hard to say, and that's kind of the whole point. It's about the painful realisation that feelings within a relationship can change. That moment when you notice the romance has faded and what's left is something that looks more like friendship, and then the fear of losing both at once is what makes it so hard to let go. We wrote it right in the middle of that, trying to catch a whole world of emotions that had no clean answers. And honestly, we weren't looking for any.

How did you approach the writing?

Henri: We didn't try to force it into any kind of logic. Thoughts during a separation are fragmented, they jump, they contradict each other, they circle back. The second you shape that into a neat narrative, you lose the honesty of it. Then it's just another breakup song. We didn't want that.

Dennis: Putting feelings into words is so hard in moments like that, we all know that. At some point I just asked Henri honestly, what do you feel? Just say it out loud. And that's where the lyrics came from. What mattered was saying out loud, completely honestly, what actually happens inside Henri in those moments. The back and forth. The hoping and the doubting. The grief and the anger. Giving up, then fighting again. All of it at once, unresolved.

Where does the "How to feel, how to breathe, how to see" come from?

Henri: We were deep in Blood Orange's catalogue during those sessions, and a line from his feature with Sassy on "Tell Me" just wouldn't let go of us. That desperate quality, the way it keeps building and building until at the end you're almost screaming it: Tell me now. It's this completely raw begging, a plea that has nowhere else to go. That's exactly where we were. It felt completely right to let it find its way into the song.

What was recording it like?

Dennis: Intense days. The most intense we've ever spent in the studio.

Henri: I sang it straight out of the emotion. I wasn't thinking about technique. By the end we knew the song needed a harder ending. More guitar-driven, more direct. Because endlessly turning something over in your mind doesn't bring you any closer to clarity. At some point the circle has to break.

Who did you work with on the production?

Dennis: Leon Kaack produced it, and by this point he's become almost part of the band. He was close to us through all of it, not just the production but the whole situation we were going through. There were days we showed up hours late to the studio because the emotions just took over and brought Henri to a breakdown. He took all of that seriously. He was empathetic, patient in a way that felt almost endless, and he navigated us through the production with a kind of care we didn't take for granted. That we were able to finish this song at all, we owe a huge part of that to him.

What do you want the song to leave behind?

Dennis: No answers. That's on purpose. The song isn't trying to bring clarity. It's only saying that it's okay if you don't have any. Just: been there, done that, I hear you, I feel you.

How did the Artwork for the cover come together?

Henri: We spent a long time looking for artwork that would actually reflect what the song is about. A big photo shoot felt wrong. CGI felt even more wrong. Everything looked too polished, too constructed, too far from the rawness of what we were trying to say. So we sat down in a photo booth in Berlin, pulled the curtain closed, and gave each other an honest hug. That's the cover. And in a way it's exactly what the whole process looked like too, listening to each other, being there for each other, and always coming back to that honest embrace. No staging. No distance. Just the two of us in a small booth, figuring it out, like friends do sometimes. And little funny side note: We found out afterwards that Harry Styles had been in the same booth a few weeks earlier. Lol.
